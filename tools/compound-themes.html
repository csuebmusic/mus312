compound themes

Caplin's compound themes are sixteen real measures long. The compound period and the compound sentence have the functions of the simple period and sentence, built from larger units (chapter 6, p. 166).

Press any box, bracket, or cadence for its definition. Colors name the idea. Beneath them, two rows of brackets name functions at two levels, and a last bracket names the theme. Terms from the simple themes link to their definitions on the theme types page.

simple and compound

Real measures can differ from notated ones. Where R = 2N, a theme notated in sixteen measures sounds as a simple eight-measure theme, and where R = ½N, the movement as a whole can show that a theme notated in eight measures is compound. Caplin finds the distinction often unimportant: a simple theme and a compound theme have the same formal functions (pp. 166, 185–186).

Phrase and subphrase are used as defined on the theme types page. A compound sentence with one cadence is one phrase, sixteen measures long. A compound period has one phrase for each of its cadences: two where each half has one cadence, four where each half opens with an antecedent.

the compound period

A compound period is a sixteen-measure theme of compound antecedent + compound consequent. The compound antecedent closes with a weak cadence at m. 8, and the basic idea returns at m. 9 to open the compound consequent, which closes with a PAC at m. 16. Caplin sorts compound periods into three categories by the simple theme type of the compound antecedent (pp. 166, 171–172).

  1. A compound antecedent is an eight-measure sentence or hybrid that closes with a weak cadence, usually an HC, regular or reinterpreted, and rarely an IAC. It doesn't end with a simple consequent. A simple consequent would bring the basic idea back at m. 5, before the return at m. 9 that opens the compound consequent (pp. 166, 171).
  2. A compound consequent repeats the compound antecedent and closes with a PAC. Its cadence is usually more emphatic than the antecedent's, and its last four-measure unit often has an ECP. A four-measure ECP at the end of a compound period corresponds to the two-measure cadential progression under the final contrasting idea of a simple period (pp. 166, 171, 173).
  3. The basic idea returns at m. 9 to open the compound consequent, and the whole first four-measure unit usually returns with it. It doesn't come back earlier in the theme. A restatement at mm. 3–4, inside a presentation, is heard as a repetition rather than a return (pp. 171–172).
  1. Where the compound antecedent is a sentence, the compound period is two eight-measure sentences. Caplin calls it the full development of the simple period whose four-measure antecedent is a sentence (pp. 88, 172).
  2. Where the compound antecedent is compound basic idea + continuation, or compound basic idea + continuation ⇒ cadential, each half is a hybrid that opens with a compound basic idea (pp. 173–174).
  3. Where the compound antecedent is antecedent + continuation, the theme needs a weak cadence at m. 4 as well as at m. 8. The compound antecedent ends with a half cadence, regular or reinterpreted, rather than an IAC. The compound consequent's second phrase can have an ECP, and the consequent then resembles the hybrid antecedent + cadential (pp. 174–175).
  4. A reinterpreted half cadence closes an antecedent that has modulated to the dominant region and reached a PAC there. When the consequent returns at once to the home key, that PAC is heard again as a half cadence in the home key, written PAC ⇒ HC. It's rare in simple periods and more frequent in compound ones, where the PAC in the new key closes the simple theme the compound antecedent is built as (pp. 90–92, 176).

cadential distribution

With antecedent + continuation in each half, cadences that grow stronger across the theme, HC at m. 4, IAC at m. 8, HC at m. 12, PAC at m. 16, are rare. Caplin attributes this to the IAC, which rarely ends an antecedent of any kind. Other patterns appear instead, among them an HC at m. 4 followed by another at m. 8. In Ex. 6.13 Haydn avoids a third HC at m. 12 by turning the consequent's first phrase into a compound basic idea. In Ex. 6.7 Mozart writes the third HC and weakens it by moving the bass down a step as the upper voices resolve. A reinterpreted HC at m. 8 keeps the cadences in order at both levels: inside the compound antecedent it's a PAC in the dominant answering the IAC at m. 4, and across the whole theme it's an HC answered by the PAC at m. 16 (pp. 175–176, 184–185).

where the consequent begins

Some themes bring material like the basic idea at mm. 5–6, as if the compound antecedent held a four-measure consequent. In Ex. 6.12 (Haydn, Symphony No. 93 in D, iv) that material has different harmonies from the opening, and its faster harmonic rhythm suggests a continuation. The basic idea comes back with its own harmony at m. 9, and that return opens the consequent (p. 183).

double period

Laitz and Bartlette call the theme of Beethoven's Piano Sonata in A♭, Op. 26, i, a parallel interrupted double period: sixteen measures in four phrases, each half divided like a period, with a half cadence at m. 8 too weak to close a period on its own (Laitz, p. 120). Caplin analyzes the same theme, his Ex. 6.1, as a compound period whose compound antecedent is antecedent + continuation, with HCs at mm. 4 and 8. He doesn't use the term: it implies a period within a period, and he finds a simple period embedded in a larger one rare in classical themes (Caplin, p. 167).

the compound sentence

Compare Caplin, Ex. 6.2: Beethoven, Piano Concerto No. 3 in C minor, Op. 37, i, whose compound basic idea is repeated as a dominant response (p. 168).

A compound sentence is a sixteen-measure theme of compound presentation + continuation. It closes with one cadence: most often a PAC, sometimes an HC, rarely an IAC. Caplin finds it often at the opening of a symphony or a concerto and less often in a solo or duo sonata (pp. 168, 178–179).

  1. A compound presentation is a compound basic idea and its repetition over a tonic prolongation, with no cadence. The repetition is most often a dominant response to a tonic statement, less often exact, and rarely sequential (pp. 168, 178).
  2. A compound basic idea is a four-measure unit of basic idea + contrasting idea, with no cadence. It has the place in a sixteen-measure theme that a two-measure basic idea has in an eight-measure one. A compound sentence opens with it twice, and a compound period can open each half with it (pp. 173, 177).
  3. The continuation of a compound sentence has the processes of a simple continuation: fragmentation, harmonic acceleration, faster surface rhythm, sequential harmony. It fuses with cadential function to close the theme. Caplin doesn't call it a compound continuation: it's longer than a simple one but no more complex in structure (pp. 168–169, 179).
  4. Fragmentation is measured against the largest unit the presentation repeats, here the four-measure compound basic idea. The first stage usually cuts it to two-measure units, and these can fragment again to one measure. Measured against the two-measure ideas inside the compound basic idea, the units at m. 9 would continue the same grouping, and the fragmentation would be missed (pp. 168, 179–180).

statement–response or antecedent–consequent

A compound basic idea on I–V, repeated on V–I, can sound like an antecedent with an HC at m. 4 and a consequent with a PAC at m. 8, especially in root position. Most often a single progression underlies each four-measure unit, and no cadential progression closes a prolongational one. The eight measures are then one tonic prolongation, an open presentation rather than a period (p. 179).

compressed continuation

Many compound sentences compress continuation and cadential function into one four-measure unit, as in a simple sentence, often continuation ⇒ cadential over an ECP. Repeating it, or adding a postcadential unit after it, restores the sixteen measures (p. 180).

  1. A compressed continuation stated twice has eight measures of continuation and cadential function, as many as the presentation. Where the first statement ends with a deceptive cadence, the repetition reaches the PAC (pp. 180–181).
  2. A standing on the dominant can follow a compound sentence that ends with an HC, as a closing section follows a PAC, and it prolongs dominant harmony. A closing section usually has a recessive dynamic; a standing on the dominant tends to sustain the energy of ending on the dominant. After a simple sentence it's very rare (p. 182).
