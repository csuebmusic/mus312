a sentence in minor

reading a numeral in this course

A roman numeral in this course says two things at once. Its case reports the quality of the triad. Its accidental reports where the root sits against the major scale on the same tonic. Caplin writes every numeral uppercase and leaves quality to the reader; here the numeral carries it.

The case. Uppercase for a major triad, lowercase for a minor one. A diminished triad is lowercase and takes a small circle after it, an augmented triad is uppercase and takes a plus.

The accidental. The root is measured against the major scale on the same tonic, whatever the mode of the passage. A root that matches the major scale takes nothing. A root a semitone below it takes a flat, a semitone above it a sharp. So a triad built on E♭ in C is ♭III in C major and in C minor alike.

The same numerals hold when a chord is borrowed across the modes. A♭ C E♭ in C major is ♭VI, spelled and read exactly as it is in C minor, and the flat is what marks it as borrowed.

the figures

Figured-bass numbers follow the numeral and report which member of the chord is in the bass: nothing for root position, 6 for first inversion, 6/4 for second. A seventh chord takes 7, 6/5, 4/3, and 4/2 from root position downward. The cadential six-four is written Cad6/4 and resolves to V, where Caplin writes the pair as one harmony, V(6-4 5-3).
