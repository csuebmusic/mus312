part 1, counting in semitones: scales and chord construction

A pitch on the staff implies register and spelling. The same pitch on the clock only implies distance. Scale degrees run above the staff, pitch classes around the clock.

Hover a pitch in either space to find it in the other. Click any pitch on the clock to make it scale degree 1. Play sounds the example above it, in whatever key is showing.

major

musical space

The clef fixes identity and register: this line is E, that space is F. The distance between them comes from the scale. The major scale is spaced 2 2 1 2 2 2 1 in semitones, the pattern braced under the staff, with the semitones between 3̂ and 4̂ and between 7̂ and the octave.

A scale degree is the position a pitch holds in that pattern, counted from the tonic: 1̂ is the tonic, 2̂ the pitch a step above it, on to 7̂, with the octave returning to 1̂. The caret marks the number as a degree rather than a quantity. 3̂ is E in C major and G in E♭ major.

pitch-class space

A pitch class is a pitch with register and spelling stripped away: every C in every octave is pitch class 0, and C♯ and D♭ are both pitch class 1. Twelve pitch classes, numbered 0 through 11 from C, fill the octave.

Each position is one semitone from its neighbor. C to D is 0 to 2, a whole tone. E to F is 4 to 5, a semitone.

natural minor

Natural minor runs 2 1 2 2 1 2 2, with the semitones between 2̂ and ♭3̂ and between 5̂ and ♭6̂. It takes the key signature of the major scale a minor third above: A natural minor and C major share all seven pitches and differ in tonic.

A degree number implies quality as well as position, measured against the major scale on the same tonic. From 1̂ to 3̂ in major is a major third. The same span in any minor scale is a minor third, one semitone smaller, and the smaller degree takes a flat: ♭3̂. Natural minor reads 1̂ 2̂ ♭3̂ 4̂ 5̂ ♭6̂ ♭7̂, harmonic minor raises the seventh back to 7̂, and melodic minor ascending raises both to 6̂ and 7̂.

The shape is the major shape read from a different starting position. Set the tonic to A here and to C in the major scale: the two figures cover the same seven pitch classes, and the ring moves.

harmonic minor

Harmonic minor raises ♭7̂ of natural minor by a semitone to 7̂, giving a leading tone and a major V. The raised degree takes an accidental against the key signature, and the pattern runs 2 1 2 2 1 3 1. The 3 is an augmented second between ♭6̂ and 7̂.

An interval name has two parts. The number counts letter names inclusively: F up to G spans two letters and is a second, whatever accidentals either pitch has. The quality then measures the semitones against that number. A second of 1 semitone is minor, of 2 semitones major, and of 3 semitones augmented.

In A harmonic minor, ♭6̂ and 7̂ are F and G♯: adjacent letters, so a second, and 3 semitones apart, so augmented. F up to A♭ covers those same 3 semitones and spans three letters, F G A, making it a minor third. On the clock the two are identical, both a move of 3. On the staff they are different intervals: G♯ is the leading tone and pulls up to A, while A♭ pulls down to G.

The gap of three positions between ♭6̂ and 7̂ is arced on the rim, the one place in these four scales where adjacent scale members sit more than a whole tone apart.

melodic minor

Melodic minor takes two forms. Ascending, ♭6̂ and ♭7̂ both rise by a semitone to 6̂ and 7̂, giving 2 1 2 2 2 2 1: the upper four pitches match the major scale, the lower three stay minor. Descending, both revert and the scale is natural minor, written here from the octave down.

Both forms sit on one clock, the filled shape with solid dots ascending and the dashed outline with hollow dots descending. Five pitch classes are common to both. The shapes part at the sixth and seventh degrees.

Each row pairs an interval with its inversion. The numbers sum to 9, the semitones to 12. The two halves meet at the tritone, 6 semitones either way, spelled augmented fourth as C–F♯ and diminished fifth as C–G♭.

diatonic triads

Stacking two thirds on each degree of the major scale gives the seven diatonic triads. Their qualities come from where the semitones fall: major on 1̂, 4̂, and 5̂, minor on 2̂, 3̂, and 6̂, diminished on 7̂.

A triad is two thirds stacked. A minor third is 3 semitones, a major third is 4. The one on the bottom decides the quality, and the two together give the outer fifth.

A roman numeral names the degree and the quality at once: upper case for major, lower case for minor, lower case with a ring for diminished. The chord symbol under it names the same chord from its root, with a capital letter for major, a lower-case letter and a dash for minor, and a ring for diminished.

figured bass

A bass line is given figures: numbers naming the intervals to be built above each bass note, counted in scale steps rather than semitones. A 3 above C is E and a 5 above C is G, both taken from the key signature and left unaltered unless the figure says otherwise.

the bass alone

the bass realized

Keyboard style puts the bass in the left hand and the three upper voices in the right. Like SATB it has four voices against a triad's three pitches, so one pitch is doubled in every triad, here the root. A seventh chord holds four pitches and needs no doubling, though the fifth is often dropped and the root doubled in its place.

basic voice leading rules

  • A dissonant chord tone resolves down to a chord tone of the next chord. Sevenths and diminished fifths are the common cases. A chromatic note that is lowered, ♭6̂ in place of 6̂, resolves down as well, whether it comes from modal mixture or from a chromatic pre-dominant.
  • A leading tone resolves up to its target (in an inner voice it can instead leap down to supply a pitch the next chord needs). A chromatic note that is raised, ♯4̂ in place of 4̂, resolves up in the same way.
  • Both of these two categories are sensitive tones. Never double a sensitive tone.
  • Avoid parallel motion into perfect consonances: unisons, fifths, and octaves. Contrary motion is the safest approach to them.

inversions

The figures attached to a roman numeral come from the figured bass tradition: the numeral names the chord and the figures name the intervals above whatever note is in the bass, the same numbers a continuo player would read. Every chord below is C major in the first row and G dominant seventh in the second. Only the bass changes. Figures in parenthesis are the ones left off in practice.

nonchord tones

Not every note belongs to the chord under it. Above the harmony runs a surface of notes that pass between chord tones, lean on them, hold over from the chord before, or arrive ahead of the chord they belong to. Analysis names the harmony first and accounts for these separately.

passing tone

Fills the step between two chord tones a third apart, on a weak beat, while the harmony holds.

anticipation

A note of the coming chord arrives early and sounds against the chord still in force, the mirror of a suspension held over from the chord before. Common at a cadence.

neighbor tone

Steps away from a chord tone and back to it. The harmony does not change under it.

suspension

A note held past its chord into the next one, where it is a dissonance, and then resolved down by step. It runs in three stages: preparation, suspension, resolution.

accented passing tone

The same three notes with the dissonance moved onto the beat and the chord tone after it. A suspension without the preparation.

pedal

A single pitch sustained while the harmonies above it change, almost always 1̂ or 5̂ in the bass. Caplin marks it with ped.

The four suspension figures are 9–8, 7–6, and 4–3 in an upper voice, and 2–3 in the bass. What separates a suspension from an accented passing tone is the preparation: the suspended note is already sounding, as a chord tone, before it turns into a dissonance.

part 2, harmonic progressions and functions

tonal syntax

Harmonic function is the role a chord plays in a key, apart from its spelling and its roman numeral. Seven numerals collapse into four functions: tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and mediant.

Tonal syntax is the particular order in which those functions appear in tonal music. A tonic moves through a pre-dominant, which prepares a dominant, which resolves back to a tonic. That succession of functions is the phrase model. Mediants rarely drive a progression forward in the classical style, though they become structurally important late in it, in Beethoven, and through the romantic period. A mediant that joins the full phrase model falls between the tonic and the pre-dominant.

Caplin counts three functions. He places vi with the tonic, where it substitutes for I in a deceptive resolution, and treats iii as an occasional dominant substitute in progressions such as iii6–I. Labels on this page follow Laitz.

The four chords written over the F in the figured bass examples differ in quality, in dissonance, and in figure, yet each does the same work of preparing the dominant.

The seventh on vii°6 makes it half-diminished. The brackets group them by function, which cuts across the scale rather than running along it.

The sevenths on ii° and vii° are optional. The first makes a half-diminished chord and the second a fully diminished one, and each adds a 5 to the figure.

Two chords here stand outside the four functions. The minor v lacks a leading tone and works as a passing or voice-leading chord, harmonizing ♭7̂ in a bass that descends from the tonic. ♭VII most often acts as the dominant of ♭III and is labelled V/♭III when it does.

structural and prolongational diatonic harmonies

A structural harmony moves the syntax from one function to the next. A prolongational harmony extends the function already in force without moving it. Both belong to the same phrase, and most phrases have more of the second kind than the first.

Prolongational chords usually fall on weak beats and usually appear in inversion. Second-level analysis labels them by what they do rather than by their root: N for neighbor, P for passing, CL for chordal leap, arp for arpeggiation. Structural chords keep their roman numerals. Root-position V7 is structural and signals a cadence, while its inversions prolong.

Patterns are written in major. Minor takes the same shapes with its own qualities, so I–I6 becomes i–i6.

A bass line of do ti do fa sol do. The first three chords are one harmony: the V6 is a neighbor between two tonics, and the tonic holds until the F arrives.

Analysis is not a chord-by-chord translation. The first level names every sonority. The second level says which of them structure the phrase and which decorate it. A passage with fourteen chords may have four functions.

A phrase in C minor where every function is prolonged. The tonic is extended by an arpeggiating bass, i to iv6 to i6, filling the descending sixth from 1̂ to 3̂. The mediant is tonicized by its own dominant, ♭VII acting as V of ♭III, and then extended by a chordal leap in the bass. The pre-dominant is extended by the 5–6 motion that turns iv into ii°6 over a held bass. The dominant is extended by the cadential six-four that precedes it, labelled Cad6/4.

the cadential six-four

The dominant of a cadence often arrives with two dissonances above its bass. A sixth and a fourth above 5̂ fall by step to a fifth and a third, and only then is the dominant triad sounding.

Caplin writes this as one harmony, a root-position dominant with the embellishment in parentheses: V(6-4 5-3). Labels on this page follow Laitz.

cadences

Each example is a complete cadential progression: an initial tonic, a pre-dominant, the dominant, and whatever the dominant goes to. The bracket marks the cadence itself.

perfect authentic cadence

imperfect authentic cadence

A perfect authentic cadence needs three things at once: V moving to I, both chords in root position, and 1̂ in the soprano. An authentic cadence that misses any one of them is imperfect. Here both chords stand in root position and the soprano closes on 3̂.

half cadence

phrygian half cadence

A half cadence stops on the dominant. The chord is a triad: a seventh would make it a dominant owing a resolution. The phrygian half cadence is the minor-mode version, named for the semitone descent ♭6̂ to 5̂ in the bass, with iv6 as the pre-dominant and 4̂ rising to 5̂ above it.

deceptive cadence

plagal motion

A deceptive cadence sets up an authentic close and substitutes another chord for the final tonic, most often vi. The bass rises 5̂ to 6̂ where it was expected to fall to 1̂. Almost always this delays a stronger close: the cadential progression returns within a few measures and finishes, usually with a perfect authentic cadence.

The plagal IV to I arrives after a cadence has already closed the phrase. It extends the final tonic rather than producing it.

Caplin separates the cadential progression from the cadence. The progression is a harmonic pattern, and the cadence is the formal event that ends a theme, which he takes up alongside theme types. He counts no plagal cadence at all in this repertoire.

diatonic sequences

A sequence repeats a pattern of root motion. Six patterns are available: fifths, thirds, and seconds, each ascending and descending. Caplin names each by the interval between adjacent roots. Laitz names it by the two-chord unit instead, so a descending fifth answered by an ascending fourth is (−5/+4).

(−5/+4)

The most common of the six. vii° stands in root position with its root doubled: inside the sequence it passes rather than functioning as a dominant.

(−4/+2)

Each pair of chords drops the underlying harmony by a third, and the soprano descends the scale against it.

(−2)

Root position would produce parallel fifths between adjacent chords. Every chord stands in first inversion instead, and the version that appears most often adds a chain of 7–6 suspensions above the bass.

(−3/+4)

The bass holds while the soprano moves from a fifth above it to a sixth, and only then steps up. That 5–6 motion breaks the parallel fifths a stepwise ascent in root position would produce, and the first-inversion chords it generates stand in a dominant relation to the chords that follow.

(+5/−4)

Reverses the strong progression at every step. It is rare.

(+3)

The rarest of the six. It becomes usable when a passing chord falls between each pair, acting as the dominant of the harmony that follows.

A sequence suspends the syntax. Inside one, root motion alone drives the music: the vii° in the descending fifths is not a dominant, and the V that follows I in the descending thirds is not a cadence. Each chord is a link in a pattern, and the pattern drives the passage.

A sequence opens ground between the structural harmonies, moving the music away from a key or back toward one while committing to nothing along the way. The classical style puts them where instability is wanted: transitions, developments, and the stretch between one cadence and the next. When the sequence stops, the chord it lands on takes up a function again and the syntax resumes.

The tonic led to the mediant by an ascending-fifths sequence, then a cadential progression closing with a perfect authentic cadence.

part 3, chromatic harmony

applied dominant chords

A chord can be tonicized: treated for a moment as a tonic and given a dominant of its own. That dominant is an applied chord, also called a secondary dominant. Its roman numeral names the chord and the chord it points to at once: V/ii is read five of two.

Only a stable chord can be tonicized, which for triads means major or minor. In C major that leaves ii, iii, IV, V, and vi. The dominant of I is V, already in the key. The triad on 7̂ is diminished and cannot be tonicized.

Hover an applied dominant to follow it into its target.

Each applied dominant is a major triad a fifth above its target, so the diatonic triad standing on that root is raised until it is major.

  • V/ii, V/V, and V/vi stand on minor triads: the third rises
  • V/iii stands on the diminished triad on 7̂: the third and the fifth rise
  • V/IV stands on I, already major, and takes no accidental

function and applied chords

Each of the four functions occupies a space in the phrase, and a space can hold a phrase model of its own. A tonicization with more than one applied chord is an extended tonicization, and the chords it governs form an embedded phrase model.

A phrase model can use as many of the four functions as it needs, and rarely uses all of them. The dominant space takes the expansion most often, usually as the jazz ii–V–I: in C major, ii/V and V7/V arriving on V, a pre-dominant and a dominant reaching a tonic of their own.

The bracket and arrow are standard jazz notation. The upper row of functions reads the embedded model in G, the lower row the phrase in C. Heard, the model leans toward G without cadencing there: the arrival on V turns out to be a dominant, not a tonic.

An applied chord belongs to the function of the chord it points at: V/vi and V/iii to the mediant, V/ii and V/IV to the pre-dominant, V/V to the dominant. The voice leading rules hold unchanged. Chromatic harmony puts more sensitive tones on the page, and each one still resolves: raised notes up, lowered notes down, never doubled. In the phrase above, F♯ (leading tone of the applied dominant) rises to G (its target) and the chordal seventh C resolves down to B.

applied leading-tone chords

Three chords stand on 7̂:

  • diminished triad: two minor thirds, on 7̂ in major and on the raised 7̂ in minor
  • half-diminished seventh: a major third above the triad, diatonic on 7̂ in major
  • fully diminished seventh: a minor third above the triad, diatonic on the raised 7̂ in minor

The diminished triad contains two sensitive tones: the diminished fifth above the root, which needs to resolve down, and the root itself, a leading tone, which needs to resolve up to its target. Together they make the tritone between 4̂ and 7̂. The chordal seventh of the half-diminished and of the fully diminished is a sensitive tone too, and resolves down.

vii° has dominant function alongside V, so it can be applied in the same way. Its root sits a semitone below the root of its target.

The target decides which seventh to build. The fully diminished belongs to a minor tonic and the half-diminished to a major one, so a major target admits either and a minor target admits only the fully diminished. The fully diminished is the more common in both cases, and it stays fully diminished whatever the mode of the passage around it.

chromatic pre-dominants

A chromatic pre-dominant takes pre-dominant function with one or more altered scale degrees. The augmented sixth of the name is the interval between ♭6̂ and ♯4̂, and it opens outward to the octave on 5̂. The Neapolitan sixth is a major triad built on ♭2̂.

  • Italian augmented sixth, It+6: ♭6̂ 1̂ ♯4̂, with 1̂ doubled
  • French augmented sixth, Fr+6: ♭6̂ 1̂ 2̂ ♯4̂
  • German augmented sixth, Ger+6: ♭6̂ 1̂ ♭3̂ ♯4̂
  • Neapolitan sixth, N6: ♭2̂ 4̂ ♭6̂, with 4̂ in the bass

the augmented sixths

  • ♭6̂ is in the bass and resolves down to 5̂
  • ♯4̂ resolves up to 5̂
  • 1̂ is in all three chords and moves to 7̂
  • the Italian doubles 1̂, and the doubling moves to 2̂
  • the French already has 2̂, which is held, or moves to the seventh where the dominant arrives as V7
  • the German has ♭3̂, which is held into a cadential six-four

the Neapolitan sixth

  • 4̂ is in the bass and is doubled
  • the bass rises to 5̂, and the doubled 4̂ stays where it is to become the seventh of V7
  • ♭2̂, in whatever voice it falls, leaps down to 7̂
  • ♭6̂, in whatever voice it falls, resolves down to 5̂
  • the V7 arrives without its fifth

master function and prolongation chart

Two processes account for chromaticism in tonal music. Tonicization works through dominant function: a chromatic tone acts as a leading tone into the chord that follows. Modal mixture works mostly through pre-dominant function: a chord borrows a pitch from the parallel mode, and the borrowed tone keeps its scale-degree function in altered form.

Borrowing runs both ways, and minor into major is far the more common: the minor mode already has two forms of 6̂ and 7̂, while the major mode has no altered 3̂, 6̂, or 7̂ of its own. ♭6̂ is the degree most often involved. It drives by half step to 5̂, it colors every pre-dominant, and it leaves the home key standing where ♭3̂ would put the mode of the whole piece in question.

Melodic mixture changes a chord's quality and leaves its root: ii becomes ii°, ii7 becomes iiø7, IV becomes iv. Harmonic mixture changes the root itself: ♭VI, ♭III. Either takes the label it would have in the parallel minor, and neither changes the chord's function.

  • double a chromatically altered tone only where it is the root of its chord, as in ♭VI
  • approach and leave an altered tone by step, keeping the chromatic line in one voice
  • once ♭VI, iv, or ii° has entered, hold the mixture until the dominant arrives: a diatonic 6̂ before then makes a cross relation and breaks the drive to 5̂

Chromatic harmony expands steadily across the repertoire this course covers. In Haydn and Mozart mixture is local color inside a firmly diatonic frame. Schubert saturates major-mode pieces with the parallel minor far enough that the twenty-four keys start to behave like twelve major-minor keys. By the end of the nineteenth century the chromatic chords in a passage can outnumber the diatonic ones. The chart maps the tonal system, not any one period of it.

A chord borrowed from the parallel mode sits in the other column of the same box, and the function of that box is the function it keeps. The panel under each box holds the common ways that function is prolonged: the inversions, the passing and neighboring chords, and the applied chords pointing into it.

quality informs function: a guided analysis

Analysis can be daunting, but with the proper methodology you can tackle any passage. The main thing to remember: in tonal music, the quality of a chord (what it is) will always inform its function. And when you have function, you have syntax, and with syntax you will always be able to predict where the music is going. Tonal music is a predictable motion of stable musical energy becoming unstable and needing to resolve.

In the passage above, the first step is to find the quality of the chords. What are they: major triads, minor triads, what types of sevenths. This is why knowing your chords is essential, and the sooner you have this internalized, the better.

There is only one chord that does not make sense under the regular chord-finding techniques (it does not stack in thirds). When this happens, your first instinct should always be to check whether it is a chromatic pre-dominant. For that you do not need qualities of thirds, you need scale degrees.

Look at the chromatic pre-dominant charts above and you will recognize this as a French augmented sixth. This chord can only have pre-dominant function, which means the dominant should be the next chord. Look ahead. Is it? Yes, it is a G7. Dominant chords can only have dominant function, there is no other choice. You have just clarified the cadential motion for this passage.

Now look at the start, since the last chord is the same as the first. Clearly we are in C major, which clarifies most of the rest of the chords. Be methodical. Is the second chord diatonic to C? It is an A major triad, so no. Skip it for now. Is the third chord diatonic? It is a d-, so yes, that is a ii chord. Is the fourth chord diatonic? Yes, that is a C major again. Check the bass: it is in an inversion, I6. Is the fifth chord diatonic? Yes, it is that same ii chord, but now with an F in the bass, ii6.

Now the only missing function is that A major triad. Remember, quality informs function. What can an A major actually be?

  • it can be a I chord in A major. Not a good option, we are in the middle of a C major passage.
  • it can be a IV chord in E major. Also not a good option, same reason.
  • it can be a V chord in D, major or minor. This one makes more sense. If it is a V of something, the something (we call it the target) will come right after. Is there a D in the next chord? Yes, the next chord is a d- triad, which we had labelled a ii chord. You just found the V/ii.

The only thing missing now is the second level analysis: what is structural and what is prolongational.

part 4, modulation

tonicization, extended tonicization, and modulation

  • Tonicization gives a target chord a dominant of its own and moves on. It lasts a beat or a measure, and the tonicized chord keeps its function in the home key.
  • Extended tonicization gives the target more than an applied chord: a pre-dominant of its own, a cadential six-four, a phrase model embedded inside the larger one. The target is held longer and heard more strongly, but the home key still governs the musical progression.
  • Modulation goes past both. The new target stops behaving as a chord in the home key and becomes a tonic in its own right, held long enough and confirmed strongly enough to rival the key it came from.

large-scale modulation as a classical development

Baroque music does technically modulate. The movements of a dance suite are typically continuous binary (a 2-part form), where the first section moves away from the tonic towards a V in a major-mode movement or III in a minor-mode, and the second section works its way back to the original tonic. What carries these modulations, however, are tonicizations and extended tonicizations spun out without a break, one area giving way to the next. A sequence alone can do it: quit one chord early, rehear a chord in it as a pre-dominant, and the music has landed somewhere new. These modulations are small-scale, meaning, the weight of the new target is never strong enough to really rival the original tonic. This is why we often mark these as extended tonicizations, where the functions are listed in the context of the original tonic (the V never really becomes a I, in its own right).

What the classical style changes is the weight the second key carries. Home key and subordinate key become the two poles of a movement, and the subordinate key gets a stretch of the form to itself: a transition that modulates to it, a break, and a theme whose task is to close in it with a perfect authentic cadence. That cadence is what actually confirms a real large-scale modulation.

the three stages of modulation

Modulation is, in fact, a long process: the new I needs time to actually get established as a new I. It runs in three stages.

  • Harmonic destabilization of the original tonic. A seventh gets added to it, a mixture chord enters, or the harmony simply stops returning to it and starts passing through it instead. Nothing has really left the key yet, but the tonic is no longer where the music rests.
  • Tonicization and/or extended tonicization of the new target, confirmed with an authentic cadence. The target gets a dominant of its own, and then more than a dominant: a pre-dominant, a cadential six-four, a whole phrase model of its own. Somewhere in here a chord gets heard in both keys at once, and the syntax shifts under the music. The authentic cadence in the new key closes this stage, and it is also what separates a modulation from an extended tonicization.
  • The new target stays, and thus becomes the new I. The music keeps treating it as a tonic: prolonging it, drawing its pre-dominants and dominants from the new key, cadencing in it again. Without this, what we heard was really a strong tonicization that turned back.

destabilizing the tonic

There are a few ways to take the stability out of the tonic without actually leaving the key. A seventh added to the tonic triad turns it into the dominant of IV. A borrowed ♭3̂ turns it minor and opens the flat side. A tonic that only ever shows up in inversion, passed through on the way somewhere else, stops being a point of arrival at all.

the pivot chord

A pivot chord belongs to both keys at once. We approach it as a chord in the old key and leave it as a chord in the new one, meaning the sound itself never actually changes: what changes is the syntax underneath it. To find one, start at the end: locate the authentic cadence in the new key, bracket just the V and the I, and work backwards from there until you reach a chord that makes sense in both keys. Not every modulation uses a pivot, but it is a very common technique.

The submediant is the common pivot in a move to the dominant: vi6 at home is ii6 in the key of V, and the pre-dominant it becomes leads straight to the cadence that confirms the new tonic. A six-four is never a pivot. It is unstable in both keys.

closely related keys

The closely related keys of a home key are the ones built on its own consonant triads. For C major those are D minor (ii), E minor (iii), F major (IV), G major (V), and A minor (vi). No key signature among them is more than one accidental away from C major's, which keeps them within a step of it on the circle of fifths. Major keys in classicism most often modulate to V, vi, and IV. Minor keys most often modulate to ♭III, but also to v.

Because the two keys share almost all of their material, the pivot can be a plain diatonic chord. Writing out the roman numerals of both keys under the same chords and reading down the columns is the fastest way to find one: wherever the chord is the same in both keys, you have a candidate, and the best candidates are the ones that become a pre-dominant in the new key.

chromatic modulation

A chromatic modulation goes to a key that is not closely related, and the diatonic method runs out. C major and A♭ major, the key of ♭VI, have no triad in common at all. In order to find a pivot between these two keys, we must turn to modal mixture (see chart below).

Borrowing from C minor puts four chords into both keys at once, and that is what a chromatic pivot always turns out to be: a mixture chord in the key we are leaving, a plain diatonic chord in the key we are entering. The chromaticism belongs to the old key, not to the new one. It also means that mixture turning up in a passage is often the first sign that a chromatic modulation is on its way.

Keys a third away from the tonic start turning up in Haydn's slow movements (where chromatic exploration had a natural place) around 1790, at first as departures from the norm and later as an ordinary option: the G major Adagio of the Symphony No. 99 in E♭, the F♯ major Largo of the Quartet Op. 76 No. 5. Beethoven takes it up almost at once, in the E major Adagio of the Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2 No. 3, and normalizes them into large-scale modulations that govern larger forms (Sonata, Theme & Variations). ♭VI and ♭III are the chromatic harmonies composers at the cusp of Classicism and Romanticism tonicized most often. By Schubert the parallel modes are mixed freely enough that the twenty-four keys behave more like twelve major-minor keys, and the pivot itself becomes optional. Two keys can be juxtaposed directly, with no shared chord and no preparation (unprepared, or direct, modulation), or hinged on a single common tone held across the seam (common-tone modulation).
