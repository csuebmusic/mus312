counting in semitones: scales and chord construction

A pitch on the staff carries register and spelling. The same pitch on the clock carries distance. Scale degrees run above the staff, pitch classes around the clock.

Hover a pitch in either space to find it in the other. Click any pitch on the clock to make it scale degree 1.

major

musical space

The clef fixes identity and register: this line is E, that space is F. The distance between them comes from the scale. The major scale is spaced 2 2 1 2 2 2 1 in semitones, the pattern braced under the staff, with the semitones between 3̂ and 4̂ and between 7̂ and the octave.

A scale degree is the position a pitch holds in that pattern, counted from the tonic: 1̂ is the tonic, 2̂ the pitch a step above it, on to 7̂, with the octave returning to 1̂. The caret marks the number as a degree rather than a quantity. 3̂ is E in C major and G in E♭ major.

pitch-class space

A pitch class is a pitch with register and spelling stripped away: every C in every octave is pitch class 0, and C♯ and D♭ are both pitch class 1. Twelve pitch classes, numbered 0 through 11 from C, fill the octave.

Each position is one semitone from its neighbor. C to D is 0 to 2, a whole tone. E to F is 4 to 5, a semitone.

natural minor

Natural minor runs 2 1 2 2 1 2 2, with the semitones between 2̂ and ♭3̂ and between 5̂ and ♭6̂. It takes the key signature of the major scale a minor third above: A natural minor and C major share all seven pitches and differ in tonic.

A degree number carries quality as well as position, measured against the major scale on the same tonic. From 1̂ to 3̂ in major is a major third. The same span in any minor scale is a minor third, one semitone smaller, and the smaller degree takes a flat: ♭3̂. Natural minor reads 1̂ 2̂ ♭3̂ 4̂ 5̂ ♭6̂ ♭7̂, harmonic minor raises the seventh back to 7̂, and melodic minor ascending raises both to 6̂ and 7̂.

The shape is the major shape read from a different starting position. Set the tonic to A here and to C in the major scale: the two figures cover the same seven pitch classes, and the ring moves.

harmonic minor

Harmonic minor raises ♭7̂ of natural minor by a semitone to 7̂, giving a leading tone and a major V. The raised degree takes an accidental against the key signature, and the pattern runs 2 1 2 2 1 3 1. The 3 is an augmented second between ♭6̂ and 7̂.

An interval name carries two parts. The number counts letter names inclusively: F up to G spans two letters and is a second, whatever accidentals either pitch carries. The quality then measures the semitones against that number. A second of 1 semitone is minor, of 2 semitones major, and of 3 semitones augmented.

In A harmonic minor, ♭6̂ and 7̂ are F and G♯: adjacent letters, so a second, and 3 semitones apart, so augmented. F up to A♭ covers those same 3 semitones and spans three letters, F G A, making it a minor third. On the clock the two are identical, both a move of 3. On the staff they are different intervals, and the difference governs how each resolves: G♯ is the leading tone and pulls up to A, while A♭ pulls down to G.

The gap of three positions between ♭6̂ and 7̂ is arced on the rim, the one place in these four scales where adjacent scale members sit more than a whole tone apart.

melodic minor

Melodic minor takes two forms. Ascending, ♭6̂ and ♭7̂ both rise by a semitone to 6̂ and 7̂, giving 2 1 2 2 2 2 1: the upper four pitches match the major scale, the lower three stay minor. Descending, both revert and the scale is natural minor, written here from the octave down.

Both forms sit on one clock, the filled shape with solid dots ascending and the dashed outline with hollow dots descending. Five pitch classes are common to both. The shapes part at the sixth and seventh degrees.

diatonic triads

Stacking two thirds on each degree of the major scale gives the seven diatonic triads. Their qualities come from where the semitones fall: major on 1̂, 4̂, and 5̂, minor on 2̂, 3̂, and 6̂, diminished on 7̂.

A triad is two thirds stacked. A minor third is 3 semitones, a major third is 4. Which one sits on the bottom decides the quality, and the two together give the outer fifth.

A roman numeral names the degree and the quality at once: upper case for major, lower case for minor, lower case with a ring for diminished. The chord symbol under it names the same chord from its root, with a capital letter for major, a lower-case letter and a dash for minor, and a ring for diminished.

Two major thirds stacked give a fourth triad quality: 4 and 4, an outer 8. Its roman numeral is upper case with a plus. The augmented triad sits on ♭3̂ in harmonic and melodic minor.

melodic minor ascending

chord builder

Two thirds stacked give the four triads, and every combination of 3 and 4 is one of them. Three thirds stacked give the seven seventh chords: 4 and 4 and 4 would close the octave, and the other seven combinations are all in use.

figured bass

A bass line carries figures: numbers naming the intervals to be built above each bass note, counted in scale steps rather than semitones. A 3 above C is E and a 5 above C is G, both taken from the key signature and left unaltered unless the figure says otherwise.

the bass alone

the bass realized

The realization puts the bass in the left hand and the three upper voices in the right, which is called keyboard style. Like SATB, it carries four voices while a triad holds three pitches, so one pitch is doubled in every triad. The realizations here double the root. A seventh chord holds four pitches and needs no doubling, though the fifth is often dropped and the root doubled in its place.

* The full figure for a root-position triad is 5 over 3, almost always left off the page. An unfigured bass note means 5 and 3.

basic voice leading rules

  • A dissonant chord tone resolves down to a chord tone of the next chord. Sevenths and diminished fifths are the common cases. A chromatic note that is lowered, ♭6̂ in place of 6̂, resolves down as well, whether it comes from modal mixture or from a chromatic predominant.
  • A leading tone resolves up to its target (in an inner voice it can instead leap down to supply a pitch the next chord needs). A chromatic note that is raised, ♯4̂ in place of 4̂, resolves up in the same way.
  • Both of these two categories are sensitive tones. Never double a sensitive tone.
  • Avoid parallel motion into perfect consonances: unisons, fifths, and octaves. Contrary motion is the safest approach to them.

harmonic progressions and functions

tonal syntax

Harmonic function is the role a chord plays in a key, apart from its spelling and its roman numeral. Seven numerals collapse into four functions: tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and mediant.

Tonal syntax is the particular order in which those functions appear in tonal music. A tonic moves through a pre-dominant, which prepares a dominant, which resolves back to a tonic. Mediants rarely carry a progression forward in the classical style, though they become structurally important late in it, in Beethoven, and through the romantic period. A mediant that joins the full phrase model falls between the tonic and the pre-dominant.

Caplin counts three functions. He places vi with the tonic, where it substitutes for I in a deceptive resolution, and treats iii as an occasional dominant substitute in progressions such as V–iii–IV. Labels on this page follow Laitz.

A chord earns its function from where it sits in the syntax rather than from what it is built out of. The four chords written over the F in the examples above differ in quality, in dissonance, and in figure, yet each does the same work of preparing the dominant.

The diatonic triads and common seventh chords of C major, each written in the inversion in which it most often appears in tonal music: ii and vii° in first inversion, V with its seventh, and optional sevenths on ii, IV, and vii°. The seventh on vii°6 makes it half-diminished. The brackets group them by function, which cuts across the scale rather than running along it.

The same in C harmonic minor, with the alternatives the mode makes available. Degree 5 carries the minor v of natural minor and the V7 that the raised leading tone produces. The sevenths on ii° and vii° are optional. The first makes a half-diminished chord and the second a fully diminished one, and each adds a 5 to the figure.

Two chords here stand outside the four functions. The minor v lacks a leading tone and works as a passing or voice-leading chord, harmonizing ♭7̂ in a bass that descends from the tonic. ♭VII most often acts as the dominant of ♭III and is labelled V/♭III when it does.

structural and prolongational harmonies

A structural harmony moves the syntax from one function to the next. A prolongational harmony extends the function already in force without moving it. Both belong to the same phrase, and most phrases have more of the second kind than the first.

Prolongational chords usually fall on weak beats and usually appear in inversion. Second-level analysis labels them by what they do rather than by their root: N for neighbor, P for passing, CL for chordal leap, arp for arpeggiation. Structural chords keep their roman numerals. Root-position V7 is structural and signals a cadence, while its inversions prolong.

Patterns are written in major. Minor takes the same shapes with its own qualities, so I–I6 becomes i–i6.

A bass line of do ti do fa sol do. The first three chords are one harmony: the V6 is a neighbor between two tonics, and the tonic holds until the F arrives.

Analysis is not a chord-by-chord translation. The first level names every sonority. The second level says which of them carry the phrase and which decorate it. A passage with fourteen chords may have four functions.

A phrase in C minor where every function is prolonged. The tonic is extended by an arpeggiating bass, i to iv6 to i6, filling the descending sixth from 1̂ to 3̂. The mediant is tonicized by its own dominant, ♭VII acting as V of ♭III, and then extended by a chordal leap in the bass. The pre-dominant is extended by the 5–6 motion that turns iv into ii°6 over a held bass. The dominant is extended by the cadential six-four that precedes it, labelled Cad6/4.

cadences

Each example is a complete cadential progression: an initial tonic, a pre-dominant, the dominant, and whatever the dominant goes to. The bracket marks the cadence itself.

perfect authentic cadence

imperfect authentic cadence

A perfect authentic cadence needs three things at once: V moving to I, both chords in root position, and 1̂ in the soprano. An authentic cadence that misses any one of them is imperfect. Here both chords stand in root position and the soprano closes on 3̂.

half cadence

phrygian half cadence

A half cadence stops on the dominant. The chord is a triad: a seventh would make it a dominant owing a resolution. The phrygian half cadence is the minor-mode version, named for the semitone descent ♭6̂ to 5̂ in the bass, with iv6 as the pre-dominant and 4̂ rising to 5̂ above it.

deceptive cadence

plagal motion

A deceptive cadence sets up an authentic close and substitutes another chord for the final tonic, most often vi. The bass rises 5̂ to 6̂ where it was expected to fall to 1̂. Almost always this delays a stronger close: the cadential progression returns within a few measures and finishes, usually with a perfect authentic cadence.

The plagal IV to I arrives after a cadence has already closed the phrase. It extends the final tonic rather than producing it.

Caplin separates the cadential progression from the cadence. The progression is a harmonic pattern, and the cadence is the formal event that ends a theme, which he takes up alongside theme types. He counts no plagal cadence at all in this repertoire.

diatonic sequences

A sequence repeats a pattern of root motion. Six patterns are available: fifths, thirds, and seconds, each ascending and descending. Caplin names each by the interval between adjacent roots. Laitz names it by the two-chord unit instead, so a descending fifth answered by an ascending fourth is (−5/+4).

descending fifths (−5/+4)

The most common of the six. vii° stands in root position with its root doubled: inside the sequence it passes rather than functioning as a dominant.

descending thirds (−4/+2), the Pachelbel

Each pair of chords drops the underlying harmony by a third, and the soprano descends the scale against it. Also called the descending 5–6.

descending seconds

Root position would produce parallel fifths between adjacent chords. Every chord stands in first inversion instead, and the version that appears most often adds a chain of 7–6 suspensions above the bass.

ascending seconds (−3/+4), the ascending 5–6

The bass holds while the soprano moves from a fifth above it to a sixth, and only then steps up. That 5–6 motion breaks the parallel fifths a stepwise ascent in root position would produce, and the first-inversion chords it generates stand in a dominant relation to the chords that follow.

ascending fifths (+5/−4)

Reverses the strong progression at every step, and rare for it.

ascending thirds

The rarest of the six. It becomes usable when a passing chord falls between each pair, acting as the dominant of the harmony that follows.
