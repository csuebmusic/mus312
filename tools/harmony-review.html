counting in semitones: scales and chord construction

A pitch on the staff carries register and spelling. The same pitch on the clock carries distance. Scale degrees run above the staff, pitch classes around the clock.

Hover a pitch in either space to find it in the other. Click any pitch on the clock to make it scale degree 1.

major

musical space

The clef fixes identity and register: this line is E, that space is F. The distance between them comes from the scale. The major scale is spaced 2 2 1 2 2 2 1 in semitones, the pattern braced under the staff, with the semitones between 3̂ and 4̂ and between 7̂ and the octave.

A scale degree is the position a pitch holds in that pattern, counted from the tonic: 1̂ is the tonic, 2̂ the pitch a step above it, on to 7̂, with the octave returning to 1̂. The caret marks the number as a degree rather than a quantity. 3̂ is E in C major and G in E♭ major.

pitch-class space

A pitch class is a pitch with register and spelling stripped away: every C in every octave is pitch class 0, and C♯ and D♭ are both pitch class 1. Twelve pitch classes, numbered 0 through 11 from C, fill the octave.

Each position is one semitone from its neighbor. C to D is 0 to 2: one, two, a whole tone. E to F is 4 to 5: one, a semitone.

natural minor

Natural minor runs 2 1 2 2 1 2 2, with the semitones between 2̂ and ♭3̂ and between 5̂ and ♭6̂. It takes the key signature of the major scale a minor third above: A natural minor and C major share all seven pitches and differ in tonic.

A degree number carries quality as well as position, measured against the major scale on the same tonic. From 1̂ to 3̂ in major is a major third. The same span in any minor scale is a minor third, one semitone smaller, and the smaller degree takes a flat: ♭3̂. Natural minor reads 1̂ 2̂ ♭3̂ 4̂ 5̂ ♭6̂ ♭7̂, harmonic minor raises the seventh back to 7̂, and melodic minor ascending raises both to 6̂ and 7̂.

The shape is the major shape read from a different starting position. Set the tonic to A here and to C in the major scale: the two figures cover the same seven pitch classes, and the ring moves.

harmonic minor

Harmonic minor raises ♭7̂ of natural minor by a semitone to 7̂, giving a leading tone and a major V. The raised degree takes an accidental against the key signature, and the pattern runs 2 1 2 2 1 3 1. The 3 is an augmented second between ♭6̂ and 7̂.

An interval name carries two parts. The number counts letter names inclusively: F up to G spans two letters and is a second, whatever accidentals either pitch carries. The quality then measures the semitones against that number. A second of 1 semitone is minor, of 2 semitones major, and of 3 semitones augmented.

In A harmonic minor, ♭6̂ and 7̂ are F and G♯: adjacent letters, so a second, and 3 semitones apart, so augmented. F up to A♭ covers those same 3 semitones and spans three letters, F G A, making it a minor third. On the clock the two are identical, both a move of 3. On the staff they are different intervals, and the difference governs how each resolves: G♯ is the leading tone and pulls up to A, while A♭ pulls down to G.

The gap of three positions between ♭6̂ and 7̂ is arced on the rim, the one place in these four scales where adjacent scale members sit more than a whole tone apart. Click any pitch: the gap turns with the shape.

melodic minor

Melodic minor takes two forms. Ascending, ♭6̂ and ♭7̂ both rise by a semitone to 6̂ and 7̂, giving 2 1 2 2 2 2 1: the upper four pitches match the major scale, the lower three stay minor. Descending, both revert and the scale is natural minor, written here from the octave down.

Both forms sit on one clock, the filled shape with solid dots ascending and the dashed outline with hollow dots descending. Five pitch classes are common to both. The shapes part at the sixth and seventh degrees.

diatonic triads

Stacking two thirds on each degree of the major scale gives the seven diatonic triads. Their qualities come from where the semitones fall: major on 1̂, 4̂, and 5̂, minor on 2̂, 3̂, and 6̂, diminished on 7̂.

A triad is two thirds stacked. A minor third is 3 semitones, a major third is 4. Which one sits on the bottom decides the quality, and the two together give the outer fifth.

A roman numeral names the degree and the quality at once: upper case for major, lower case for minor, lower case with a ring for diminished. The chord symbol under it names the same chord from its root, with a capital letter for major, a lower-case letter and a dash for minor, and a ring for diminished.

melodic minor ascending

chord builder

Two thirds stacked give the four triads, and every combination of 3 and 4 is one of them. Three thirds stacked give the seven seventh chords: 4 and 4 and 4 would close the octave, and the other seven combinations are all in use.

figured bass

A bass line carries figures: numbers naming the intervals to be built above each bass note, counted in scale steps rather than semitones. A 3 above C is E and a 5 above C is G, both taken from the key signature and left unaltered unless the figure says otherwise.

the bass alone

the bass realized

The realization puts the bass in the left hand and the three upper voices in the right, which is called keyboard style. Like SATB, it carries four voices while a triad holds three pitches, so one pitch is doubled in every triad. The realizations here double the root, the simplest choice and not always the best one. A seventh chord holds four pitches and needs no doubling, though the fifth is often dropped and the root doubled in its place.

* The full figure for a root-position triad is 3 over 5, and it is almost always left off the page. An unfigured bass note means 3 and 5, which is why the figures above are in parenthesis.

The A♭ on the left is ♭6̂, borrowed from C minor, and resolves to G. The two dominant sevenths are voiced differently. basic voice leading rules

  • A dissonant chord tone resolves down to a chord tone of the next chord. Sevenths and diminished fifths are the common cases. A chromatic note that is lowered, ♭6̂ in place of 6̂, resolves down as well, whether it comes from modal mixture or from a chromatic predominant.
  • A leading tone resolves up to its target (in an inner voice it can instead leap down to supply a pitch the next chord needs). A chromatic note that is raised, ♯4̂ in place of 4̂, resolves up in the same way.
  • Both of these two categories are sensitive tones. Never double a sensitive tone.
  • Avoid parallel motion into perfect consonances: unisons, fifths, and octaves. Contrary motion is the safest approach to them.

harmonic progressions and functions

tonal syntax

Harmonic function is the role a chord plays in a key, apart from its spelling and its roman numeral. Seven numerals collapse into four functions: tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and mediant.

Tonal syntax is the particular order those functions appear in in tonal music. A tonic moves through a pre-dominant, which prepares a dominant, which resolves back to a tonic. Mediants rarely carry a progression forward in the classical style, though they become structurally important late in it, in Beethoven, and through the romantic period. A mediant that joins the full phrase model falls between the tonic and the pre-dominant.

A chord earns its function from where it goes in the syntax, from mostly a long history of musical tradition, not from what it is built out of. The four chords written over the F in the examples above differ in quality, in dissonance, and in figure, yet each does the same work of preparing the dominant.

the seven triads by function

The diatonic triads of C major, each written in the inversion it most often takes: ii and vii° in first inversion, V with its seventh, and an optional seventh on IV. The brackets group them by function, which cuts across the scale rather than running along it.

iii and vi form one category. Each sits a third from the tonic, one above and one below, and each shares two of its three pitches with it. They substitute rather than drive the syntax forward.
