mus 312 · theme types

simple themes

Caplin's simple themes fill eight real measures and are built from two-measure ideas. The sentence and the period are the two fundamental types (Caplin, chapters 2 and 3). The hybrids combine functions of both (chapter 4). Chapter 5 adds the deviations that lengthen or shorten a theme and the functions that frame it.

Press any box, bracket, or cadence for its definition. Colors name the idea. The brackets beneath name the function each group of ideas takes and the theme they make.

phrase and subphrase

A phrase runs to a cadence, weak or strong, from the start of a theme or from the cadence before it. A functional unit that doesn't span a phrase on its own is a subphrase. A sentence is one phrase made of two subphrases. A period is two phrases.

Caplin calls every one of these four-measure units a phrase and their functions phrase functions, and he rejects the rule that a phrase closes with a cadence (p. 60). A unit his analyses label a presentation phrase is, in this course, a presentation, and a presentation is a subphrase.

A cadence closes a phrase. The HC and the IAC are weak cadences and the PAC is strong. Caplin ranks them by type alone, whatever their meter or dynamics: the HC weakest, the IAC between, the PAC strongest (p. 80). The boxed label stands where the final harmony of the cadential progression arrives, on the downbeat of m. 4 or m. 8 in these models (p. 58).

the ideas

A basic idea b.i. is the two-measure unit that opens a theme. It states the theme's characteristic melodic material, usually several motives, and prolongs tonic harmony, most often in root position. Two measures means two metrical downbeats: the idea often begins on an upbeat and ends before its second bar line (pp. 38–40).

A repetition b.i. restates the basic idea immediately, with nothing between the two statements. Caplin sorts repetitions into three types by their harmony rather than their melody. In an exact repetition the harmony is essentially the same as the original's, allowing minor variants, and the melody can be varied or even transposed to other scale degrees that harmony supports. In a statement–response repetition the statement, a tonic version, begins on I, and the response, a dominant version, begins on V. The response's melody is often transposed by a step, and IV occasionally takes the place of V. In a sequential repetition, the least common type, the whole idea (melody, harmony, and accompaniment) is transposed by the same interval in the same direction: the original is the model and the transposition the sequence. Sequential repetition is rare in a presentation, which has to prolong the tonic, and where it occurs the broader tonic prolongation emerges later in the theme. A melody that rises by a step can belong to either statement–response or sequential repetition, and the harmony tells them apart (pp. 36, 41–45).

A restart b.i. brings the basic idea back at the start of the consequent, after the antecedent's contrasting idea. Caplin calls a restatement that follows intervening material a return, and one that follows immediately a repetition (p. 83). Most often the restart is exact: the basic idea comes back over the harmony it had in mm. 1–2, even when its melody is heavily varied. Occasionally it's a dominant version answering the antecedent's tonic version, which gives the period a statement–response design. A sequential restart, usually a step higher in the supertonic region, is less stable (pp. 80–83).

A contrasting idea c.i. is a two-measure unit that follows a basic idea and isn't heard as its repetition. The contrast can be striking or slight, sometimes only a change of contour, and where the two ideas share motives the harmony tells them apart. In an antecedent or a consequent it closes with a cadential progression, and it often shows continuation processes: fragmentation, faster harmonic rhythm, a hint of sequence (pp. 77–79).

The opening of a continuation frag. / accel. / seq. takes one or more of Caplin's four continuation processes, and none of them is required. Fragmentation breaks the two-measure units into shorter ones, usually one measure, then half a measure. It concerns length alone, and a fragment can take its motives from the basic idea or bring new ones. Harmonic acceleration changes harmony faster than the presentation did. Surface rhythm grows busier, with shorter note values or more of them. Sequential harmony moves by a consistent root motion, sometimes with model–sequence technique. A continuation that keeps the two-measure grouping of the ideas before it usually shows harmonic acceleration in place of fragmentation (pp. 47–55, 105–106).

A cadential idea cad. closes a theme over a cadential progression, usually in mm. 7–8. Its melody tends to be conventional and falling. The characteristic motives of the basic idea have been liquidated by this point, eliminated gradually until only formulaic material remains (pp. 55–57).

An expanded cadential progression ECP is a single cadential progression stretched across a whole four-measure unit, beginning in its first measure (p. 61).

the sentence

A sentence is an eight-measure theme of presentation + continuation. It closes with one cadence at m. 8, of any of the three types, and it's one phrase (pp. 33–36).

  1. A presentation is a basic idea and its repetition over a tonic prolongation, with no cadence. Repeating an opening idea reinforces the sense of beginning, and a presentation lacks the cadential progression a cadence needs. When the repetition is a dominant version, the tonic prolongation may not be complete until the downbeat of m. 5 (pp. 36, 45–46, 60).
  2. A continuation takes the theme out of the stability the presentation established. It opens with one or more of the continuation processes and ends with a cadential idea: a sentence's continuation fuses continuation and cadential function in one unit. It closes most often with a PAC, sometimes an HC, rarely an IAC. Where an ECP supports the whole unit, Caplin writes continuation ⇒ cadential, the arrow read as “becomes” (pp. 36, 47–48, 60–61).

the period

A period is an eight-measure theme of antecedent + consequent: a weak cadence at m. 4, answered by a stronger one at m. 8 after the basic idea restarts. It's two phrases. Caplin reserves the term for themes whose second phrase opens with the basic idea. A theme whose second half starts with new material is a hybrid rather than a contrasting period (pp. 73–76).

  1. An antecedent is a basic idea followed by a contrasting idea that closes with a weak cadence, usually an HC, sometimes an IAC. A PAC can't close it (pp. 74, 79–80).
  2. A consequent begins with the restart of the basic idea, then a contrasting idea that closes with a stronger cadence: almost always a PAC, rarely an IAC after an antecedent's HC. The consequent's contrasting idea often resembles the antecedent's, and its cadential progression often begins earlier (pp. 74, 80–85).

the hybrids

Caplin names four hybrids, each by its two functions. None opens with a presentation. Two open with an antecedent and two with a compound basic idea (pp. 99–101).

  1. Antecedent + continuation begins like a period and ends like a sentence. It's two phrases. Caplin finds it the most frequent hybrid, and its continuation almost always closes with a PAC (pp. 100–101, 105).
  2. Antecedent + cadential follows the antecedent with a four-measure cadential unit. It's two phrases and rarely modulates (pp. 101, 106–107).
  3. Compound basic idea + continuation resembles a sentence, with a contrasting idea in mm. 3–4 where the sentence repeats its basic idea. It's one phrase, and its continuation is a subphrase (pp. 101–102).
  4. Compound basic idea + consequent resembles a period without the weak cadence at m. 4. It's one phrase, and its consequent is a subphrase. Caplin finds it infrequent (pp. 102–103).

the functions

A formal function is the specific way a passage expresses a general quality of time: beginning, being in the middle, ending, and, around a unit, coming before its beginning or after its end (glossary, p. 707). Caplin calls the first three initiating, medial, and concluding. A basic idea is heard as a beginning, a continuation as the middle of a theme, a cadential idea as its end (pp. 47, 103).

A function belongs to a level. Idea functions name what an idea does inside a phrase, and phrase functions what a four-measure unit does inside a theme. Later chapters add thematic functions, such as main theme and subordinate theme, and section functions, such as exposition and recapitulation (glossary, p. 707). Caplin names the second level phrase functions whatever the unit's cadence. In this course a unit that takes a phrase function is a phrase or a subphrase by the rule under phrase and subphrase.

Function and grouping are separate questions. Idea and phrase are terms of grouping structure: how long a unit is and where it starts and stops. Basic idea and presentation are terms of function: what the unit does in the time of the theme (p. 46). In each diagram the bracket names the function and the caption under it names the grouping.

Function is also separate from type. Sentence, period, and the hybrids are theme types, each a conventional set of functions. A type has no fixed place in time. A function is a place in time. A theme type takes on a function at a higher level, as when a sentence is a movement's main theme (p. 73).

One unit can take two functions at once, which Caplin calls fusion. A sentence's continuation is medial and concluding together (pp. 35, 60). A function can also change as the music goes on. Retrospective reinterpretation hears a unit again in light of what follows, written ⇒ and read “becomes.” Continuation ⇒ cadential is a unit expected as a continuation and heard, once its ECP is clear, as cadential (p. 61).

Every conventional theme type orders its functions beginning, middle, end. Consequent + presentation or continuation + antecedent would put an ending before a beginning, and no theme type does (p. 103).

  1. A compound basic idea is a basic idea followed by a contrasting idea with no cadence, usually over a tonic prolongation. Its ideas resemble an antecedent's and its harmony a presentation's. Caplin hears it as an initiating function like a basic idea, one level up, at four measures (pp. 101–102, 107–108).
  2. A cadential unit is four measures over an ECP, with few or none of the continuation processes. It follows an antecedent (pp. 101, 106).

phrase deviations

A theme's units can run longer or shorter than the model, and its ideas can be altered. Caplin describes each deviation by the function it acts on. A deviation departs from the model for a compositional purpose, and the word isn't a criticism. It isn't Hepokoski and Darcy's deformation either, which stretches a norm to its limits or past them. Deviations are routine throughout the classical repertoire (pp. 123–124).

  1. A function is extended when material is added to stretch it out in time, more than the function needs. Deleting the addition usually leaves a normative version. Extensions often repeat part of an idea, sometimes softer, as an echo. They add fragments to a continuation, often over a sequential progression. They repeat continuation or cadential material after a cadential deviation, or after an IAC to reach a PAC (pp. 124, 137–138).
  2. A function is expanded when its members are lengthened from inside. Cadential function is expanded most often, as in the ECP. Initiating functions can be expanded too, as when a hemiola stretches a basic idea to three measures. Continuation, with its fragmentation and harmonic acceleration, is less suited to expansion. Caplin's comparison: extension ties extra balloons together, and expansion blows one balloon up larger (pp. 125–127).
  3. A function is compressed when its members are shorter than the norm, as when a continuation fits its fragmentation, harmonic acceleration, and cadence into two measures. A normative version is harder to reconstruct than after the other deviations (p. 128).
  4. Interpolated material is new and unrelated, inserted between two functions that would otherwise follow each other. It belongs to neither and can be removed without disturbing the syntax. Extension is far more common. Where added material still expresses the ongoing function, even with new material, it's an extension (pp. 127, 139–140).

A deviation can keep the phrases symmetrical, 5 + 5 or 6 + 6, or make them asymmetrical, 4 + 6. In an asymmetrical theme the first phrase almost always keeps its four measures. A period usually alters both phrases the same way, even with different techniques. Two 5-measure phrases can still sound asymmetrical when the cadences divide the theme 4 + 6 (p. 140).

cadential deviations

Three cadential deviations deny a promised authentic cadence. A half cadence doesn't take them. After one, the music usually backs up to earlier material and tries again. Occasionally new material follows instead (p. 129).

  1. In a deceptive cadence the final root-position tonic is replaced, usually by VI, sometimes by I6. The replacing event groups backward: it's still the goal of the cadential progression. Caplin keeps the term for a moment that's genuinely cadential, where turning the VI into I would give a real cadence. Any other V–VI is a deceptive resolution (pp. 130–131, 141–142).
  2. In an evaded cadence the expected final tonic never arrives as a goal. What sounds at that moment begins the next group, usually backing up to repeat continuation or cadential material. The forward grouping makes the evasion, even when V moves to a root-position I: that tonic is heard only as a beginning. Caplin marks the evasion with a double slash through the cadential bracket, drawn // between the boxes here (pp. 131–132, 142).
  3. An abandoned cadence goes wrong at the dominant, which is inverted from the start, inverted along the way, or left out. Sometimes a single moment of denied arrival can be named. Sometimes the progression turns prolongational, and a new cadential progression, or further tonic prolongation, follows. A promised authentic cadence can even end up as an HC (pp. 132, 143–144).

Janet Schmalfeldt's term, the one more time technique, names the backing up after an evaded cadence to try for the cadence again. The same return can follow an IAC, a deceptive cadence, or an abandoned cadence. Caplin hears it most strongly after an evaded cadence, which breaks the grouping (p. 143).

framing functions

A theme runs from the start of its basic idea to its cadence. A thematic introduction can stand before the beginning, and postcadential material after the end. Caplin hears these framing functions as belonging to the theme they frame, the way taking a seat before class and packing up after it belong to the class without falling inside its hours (pp. 133, 135–136).

  1. A thematic introduction is short, rarely more than two measures and sometimes a chord or two. Its motivic content is minimal, so it doesn't sound like a basic idea, and it's often accompaniment that continues into the theme. Most prolong root-position tonic. It typically builds a progressive dynamic, Wallace Berry's term for a buildup of energy and anticipation through texture, rhythm, and intensity. It isn't the slow introduction that precedes a sonata exposition (pp. 133–134, 145).
  2. A closing section follows a PAC and prolongs the tonic harmony and the tonic scale degree the cadence reached. It can begin after the cadence or elide with it. It's a series of codettas, and successive codettas often get shorter. Many closing sections have a recessive dynamic, Berry's term for a dissolving of tension, and some sustain or raise the energy of the cadence (pp. 134–135, 145–146).
  3. A standing on the dominant follows a half cadence and prolongs dominant harmony throughout, often with ideas that fragment. It rarely follows the HC of an antecedent. Simple themes seldom use it, and Caplin returns to it in later chapters (p. 135).

A codetta runs from a single chord to a four-measure phrase. Its harmony is usually a tonic prolongation, often over a tonic pedal, and emphasis on IV, even a tonicized IV, is common. Its melody circles 1̂ or ends on it and avoids opening new melodic space. A cadential progression can support a codetta, but inside a closing section it isn't a true cadence. The last measures of a piece are almost always codettas after an earlier PAC, and most so-called plagal cadences are codettas (pp. 134–135, 147–154).

A four-measure codetta can close with its own cadential idea, a cadence of limited scope: cadential inside the codetta, postcadential for the theme. Caplin sets its label in parentheses rather than a box. Codettas often join by melodic overlap: one codetta's melodic goal falls on the downbeat that begins the next, without that whole measure belonging to the first, as it would in an elision (pp. 155–156).
