mus 312 · theme types

sentence, period, and hybrids

Caplin's simple themes fill eight real measures and are built from two-measure ideas. The sentence and the period are the two fundamental types (Caplin, chapters 2 and 3). The hybrids combine functions of both (chapter 4).

Press any box, bracket, or cadence for its definition. Colors name the idea. The brackets beneath name the function each group of ideas takes and the theme they make.

phrase and subphrase

A phrase runs to a cadence, weak or strong, from the start of a theme or from the cadence before it. A functional unit that doesn't span a phrase on its own is a subphrase. A sentence is one phrase made of two subphrases. A period is two phrases.

Caplin calls every one of these four-measure units a phrase and their functions phrase functions, and he rejects the rule that a phrase closes with a cadence (p. 60). A unit his analyses label a presentation phrase is, in this course, a presentation, and a presentation is a subphrase.

A cadence closes a phrase. The HC and the IAC are weak cadences and the PAC is strong. Caplin ranks them by type alone, whatever their meter or dynamics: the HC weakest, the IAC between, the PAC strongest (p. 80). The boxed label stands where the final harmony of the cadential progression arrives, on the downbeat of m. 4 or m. 8 in these models (p. 58).

the ideas

A basic idea b.i. is the two-measure unit that opens a theme. It states the theme's characteristic melodic material, usually several motives, and prolongs tonic harmony, most often in root position. Two measures means two metrical downbeats: the idea often begins on an upbeat and ends before its second bar line (pp. 38–40).

A repetition b.i. restates the basic idea immediately, with nothing between the two statements. Caplin sorts repetitions into three types by their harmony rather than their melody. In an exact repetition the harmony is essentially the same as the original's, allowing minor variants, and the melody can be varied or even transposed to other scale degrees that harmony supports. In a statement–response repetition the statement, a tonic version, begins on I, and the response, a dominant version, begins on V. The response's melody is often transposed by a step, and IV occasionally takes the place of V. In a sequential repetition, the least common type, the whole idea (melody, harmony, and accompaniment) is transposed by the same interval in the same direction: the original is the model and the transposition the sequence. Sequential repetition is rare in a presentation, which has to prolong the tonic, and where it occurs the broader tonic prolongation emerges later in the theme. A melody that rises by a step can belong to either statement–response or sequential repetition, and the harmony tells them apart (pp. 36, 41–45).

A restart b.i. brings the basic idea back at the start of the consequent, after the antecedent's contrasting idea. Caplin calls a restatement that follows intervening material a return, and one that follows immediately a repetition (p. 83). Most often the restart is exact: the basic idea comes back over the harmony it had in mm. 1–2, even when its melody is heavily varied. Occasionally it's a dominant version answering the antecedent's tonic version, which gives the period a statement–response design. A sequential restart, usually a step higher in the supertonic region, is less stable (pp. 80–83).

A contrasting idea c.i. is a two-measure unit that follows a basic idea and isn't heard as its repetition. The contrast can be striking or slight, sometimes only a change of contour, and where the two ideas share motives the harmony tells them apart. In an antecedent or a consequent it closes with a cadential progression, and it often shows continuation processes: fragmentation, faster harmonic rhythm, a hint of sequence (pp. 77–79).

The opening of a continuation frag. / accel. / seq. takes one or more of Caplin's four continuation processes, and none of them is required. Fragmentation breaks the two-measure units into shorter ones, usually one measure, then half a measure. It concerns length alone, and a fragment can take its motives from the basic idea or bring new ones. Harmonic acceleration changes harmony faster than the presentation did. Surface rhythm grows busier, with shorter note values or more of them. Sequential harmony moves by a consistent root motion, sometimes with model–sequence technique. A continuation that keeps the two-measure grouping of the ideas before it usually shows harmonic acceleration in place of fragmentation (pp. 47–55, 105–106).

A cadential idea cad. closes a theme over a cadential progression, usually in mm. 7–8. Its melody tends to be conventional and falling. The characteristic motives of the basic idea have been liquidated by this point, eliminated gradually until only formulaic material remains (pp. 55–57).

An expanded cadential progression ECP is a single cadential progression stretched across a whole four-measure unit, beginning in its first measure (p. 61).

the sentence

A sentence is an eight-measure theme of presentation + continuation. It closes with one cadence at m. 8, of any of the three types, and it's one phrase (pp. 33–36).

  1. A presentation is a basic idea and its repetition over a tonic prolongation, with no cadence. Repeating an opening idea reinforces the sense of beginning, and a presentation lacks the cadential progression a cadence needs. When the repetition is a dominant version, the tonic prolongation may not be complete until the downbeat of m. 5 (pp. 36, 45–46, 60).
  2. A continuation takes the theme out of the stability the presentation established. It opens with one or more of the continuation processes and ends with a cadential idea: a sentence's continuation fuses continuation and cadential function in one unit. It closes most often with a PAC, sometimes an HC, rarely an IAC. Where an ECP supports the whole unit, Caplin writes continuation ⇒ cadential, the arrow read as “becomes” (pp. 36, 47–48, 60–61).

the period

A period is an eight-measure theme of antecedent + consequent: a weak cadence at m. 4, answered by a stronger one at m. 8 after the basic idea restarts. It's two phrases. Caplin reserves the term for themes whose second phrase opens with the basic idea. A theme whose second half starts with new material is a hybrid rather than a contrasting period (pp. 73–76).

  1. An antecedent is a basic idea followed by a contrasting idea that closes with a weak cadence, usually an HC, sometimes an IAC. A PAC can't close it (pp. 74, 79–80).
  2. A consequent begins with the restart of the basic idea, then a contrasting idea that closes with a stronger cadence: almost always a PAC, rarely an IAC after an antecedent's HC. The consequent's contrasting idea often resembles the antecedent's, and its cadential progression often begins earlier (pp. 74, 80–85).

the hybrids

Caplin names four hybrids, each by its two functions. None opens with a presentation. Two open with an antecedent and two with a compound basic idea (pp. 99–101).

Antecedent + continuation begins like a period and ends like a sentence. It's two phrases. Caplin finds it the most frequent hybrid, and its continuation almost always closes with a PAC (pp. 100–101, 105).

Antecedent + cadential follows the antecedent with a four-measure cadential unit. It's two phrases and rarely modulates (pp. 101, 106–107).

Compound basic idea + continuation resembles a sentence, with a contrasting idea in mm. 3–4 where the sentence repeats its basic idea. It's one phrase, and its continuation is a subphrase (pp. 101–102).

Compound basic idea + consequent resembles a period without the weak cadence at m. 4. It's one phrase, and its consequent is a subphrase. Caplin finds it infrequent (pp. 102–103).

the functions

A compound basic idea is a basic idea followed by a contrasting idea with no cadence, usually over a tonic prolongation. Its ideas resemble an antecedent's and its harmony a presentation's. Caplin reads it as a basic idea at the four-measure level (pp. 101–102, 107).

A cadential unit is four measures over an ECP, with few or none of the continuation processes. It follows an antecedent (pp. 101, 106).

Caplin groups the functions by their place in time. Presentation, antecedent, and compound basic idea initiate. Continuation is medial. Cadential and consequent conclude. Each theme type orders them beginning, middle, end (p. 103).
