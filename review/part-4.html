1, tonicization, extended tonicization, modulation

tonicization, extended tonicization, and modulation

Sort each passage into one of the three, and say what decides it.

  1. In a phrase in C major, a D7 chord falls on the second beat and G major follows on the third. The phrase closes I – ii6 – V7 – I in C.
  2. A phrase in C major reaches ii/V and V7/V, arrives on G, and then takes up a pre-dominant and cadences in C.
  3. A sequence quits C, a chord in it is reheard as a pre-dominant in G, and an authentic cadence lands in G. The music then works its way back to C without cadencing in G again.
  4. A transition modulates and breaks off. A new theme closes with a perfect authentic cadence in G, and the music that follows prolongs G, draws its pre-dominants and dominants from G, and cadences in G again.
  1. Tonicization. The applied dominant lasts a beat and the G keeps its function as V in C.
  2. Extended tonicization. The target gets a phrase model of its own, and the home key still governs: the arrival on G turns out to be a dominant.
  3. Extended tonicization. The authentic cadence closes the second stage, and nothing after it treats G as a tonic.
  4. Modulation. All three stages are present, and the third is what separates this from item 3.

Baroque continuous binary movements work largely through the shape of item 3. Their moves to V or ♭III are often read as extended tonicizations.

2, the three stages

the three stages of modulation

Name the stage each event belongs to.

  1. A seventh is added to the tonic triad.
  2. A chord is heard in both keys at once and the syntax shifts under it.
  3. The new tonic is prolonged and cadences a second time.
  4. An authentic cadence lands in the new key.
  5. ♭3̂ is borrowed and the tonic turns minor.
  6. The target acquires a pre-dominant and a cadential six-four of its own.
  1. One. The tonic becomes the dominant seventh of IV and stops being a resting point.
  2. Two.
  3. Three.
  4. Two, and it closes that stage.
  5. One.
  6. Two.

3, destabilizing the tonic

destabilizing the tonic

Write each of the three in D major, in four voices, and name what each one opens.

  1. A seventh added to the tonic.
  2. A borrowing from the parallel minor.
  3. A tonic passed through rather than arrived at.
  1. D F♯ A C, the dominant seventh of IV, moving to G major. The C resolves down to B.
  2. F♮ in place of F♯ turns the tonic minor and opens the flat side: B♭ major as ♭VI follows directly.
  3. I – V4/3 – I6 over a bass rising D E F♯. The tonic appears in inversion on the way somewhere else and stops being a point of arrival.

The closely related keys stand on the consonant triads of the home key, and no signature among them is more than one accidental away.

Major keys most often go to V, vi, and IV. Minor keys most often go to ♭III, and also to v. Laitz lists iii in place of IV.

To G major, four chords are common: C, E minor, G, and A minor. D minor, F, and B diminished have no place in G, whose triads on those letters are D major, F♯ diminished, and B minor. The strongest pivot is vi, which becomes ii in G: a pre-dominant leading straight into the cadence that confirms the new tonic. vi6 as ii6 is the common form. I as IV and iii as vi arrive further from the cadence, and V as I is the arrival itself rather than a hinge.

To A minor, all seven are common, since A natural minor holds the same seven triads. What separates the keys is E major, the V of A minor, absent from C. The strongest pivot is ii, which becomes iv in A minor. IV as ♭VI also works.

A six-four is never a pivot. It is unstable in both keys.

6, chromatic modulation

chromatic modulation

C major moves to A♭ major. Show that the two keys share no triad. Write the triads of C minor against those of A♭ major, mark the chords common to both, and pick the pivot. Then name the two ways a distant key is reached with no pivot at all.

C major and A♭ major have no triad in common. A♭ major holds A♭, B♭ minor, C minor, D♭, E♭, F minor, and G diminished; C major holds none of them.

Borrowing from C minor puts four chords into both keys at once. Every chromatic pivot works this way: a mixture chord in the key being left, a plain diatonic chord in the key being entered. The chromaticism belongs to the old key.

iv in C, F minor, is the pivot to take: as vi in A♭ it leads into that key's own pre-dominant and cadence. ♭VI as I is the arrival rather than a hinge, and ♭III as V reaches the new key without a pre-dominant.

Mixture appearing in a passage is often the first sign that a chromatic modulation is coming.

Without a pivot: direct, or unprepared, modulation, where the two keys are juxtaposed with nothing shared between them; and common-tone modulation, hinged on a single pitch held across the seam.
