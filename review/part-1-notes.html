mus 312 · harmony review · problem set 1

what to look at again

Six things came up across the class. Each one sends you back to a part of problem set 1 and to the section of the harmony review that covers it.

the bass note sets the inversion

One triad, three bass notes. D F♯ A in A major:

The pitches never change. What changes is which one is lowest, and that is what the figures report.

One seventh chord, four bass notes. G B D F in C major:

Read the bass first, work out what chord all four pitches make, then ask which member of that chord the bass note is.

six semitones, two spellings

Same distance, different notes. C up to F covers C D E F, four letter names, so the first is a fourth, augmented. C up to G covers five, so the second is a fifth, diminished. The accidentals can't change the size.

Count letter names first, inclusive, from the bottom note to the top. Then let the semitones set the quality. The same pair of names covers a major third against a diminished fourth, a minor third against an augmented second, and a minor seventh against an augmented sixth.

the signature is already doing the work

i and V in F♯ minor, with every accidental the page needs and no others:

Three sharps in the signature cover F, C and G wherever they fall. The only written accidental is the E♯, the raised 7̂ that makes the dominant major.

Natural minor takes no written accidental at all. Harmonic minor takes one, on 7̂. Melodic minor ascending takes two, on 6̂ and 7̂.

three triads change between the two minors

Each pair in C minor, natural form then harmonic:

♭III becomes ♭III+, v becomes V, ♭VII becomes vii°. All three contain 7̂, and raising it is the only thing that happens.

Which is why the table in part 4 has eight rows for a seven-note scale. Natural minor has a triad on ♭7̂ and none on 7̂. Harmonic minor has one on 7̂ and none on ♭7̂. Two cells stay empty.

naming one chord three ways

Part 6 asks for four things under each chord: root, quality, inversion, and the figures that inversion takes. Here is C E♭ G B♭ with E♭ in the bass, standing in B♭ major:

Three notations name that chord, and each answers a different question. As a lead-sheet symbol it's Cm7/E♭: root, quality, and the note in the bass, which is what a player reading chords off a page needs. As a figured bass it's 6 over 5 under the E♭: the intervals to be built above that bass note. As a roman numeral it's ii6/5 in B♭ major: the degree the root stands on, the quality of the chord built there, and the same figures.

A slash symbol does name the bass note, so Cm7/E♭ and ii6/5 agree about what is lowest. What the slash doesn't say is which member of the chord that note is. E♭ is the third of C E♭ G B♭, and the third of a seventh chord in the bass takes 6/5. That is the step part 6 is asking you to take: work out the chord, find the bass among its members, and name the figure that follows.

Write the quality in words: major, minor, diminished, augmented. Perfect describes fourths, fifths, octaves and unisons, and never a triad.

the same three pitches in two keys

Part 7 closes by asking which two of its twelve chords share their pitches. Numbers 4 and 9, A C E♭ with C in the bass:

The notation is identical. In B♭ major the triad stands on 7̂ and reads vii°6, resolving to the tonic a semitone above. In G minor it stands on 2̂ and reads ii°6, going on to the dominant.
