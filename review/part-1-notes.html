mus 312 · harmony review · problem set 1

what to look at again

Six things came up often enough across the class to be worth going through together. Each one points back at the part of problem set 1 it came from, and at the section of the harmony review that covers it.

the bass note belongs to the chord

In parts 6 and 7 every chord stands on a grand staff, and the note on the bass staff is part of the chord exactly as much as the three above it. Read it first. Work out what chord all four pitches make. Then ask which member of that chord the bass note is: root, third, fifth, or seventh. That answer is the inversion, and the inversion is what the figures report.

A chord whose upper voices happen to sit in a 6/4 arrangement is still in root position if the root is in the bass. In part 7, number 5, the treble reads F♯ A D and the bass is D, so the chord is D F♯ A with its root lowest: IV, no figure.

For triads the four possibilities are 5/3, 6/3 and 6/4, written plain, 6 and 6/4. For seventh chords they are 7/5/3, 6/5, 4/3 and 4/2.

six semitones has two names

An augmented fourth and a diminished fifth cover the same distance. So do a major third and a diminished fourth, a minor third and an augmented second, a minor seventh and an augmented sixth. Counting semitones gives you the quality and nothing else.

Count letter names first, from the bottom note to the top, inclusive. That gives you the size, and the accidentals can't change it: C up to F is C D E F, four letter names, so it's some kind of fourth however either note is spelled. C up to G is five letter names, so it's some kind of fifth. Once the size is settled, the semitone count sets the quality.

the key signature stays in force

An accidental in front of a note is for what the scale takes beyond the signature. F♯ minor with three sharps needs no sharp written on F, G or C. E♭ minor with six flats needs no flat written on any of its six.

Write only the alterations and the page shows what's happening: one E♯ in F♯ harmonic minor, a D♯ and an E♯ in melodic, a D♮ in E♭ harmonic minor, a C♮ and a D♮ in E♭ melodic. Natural minor takes none at all.

minor has eight rows for seven notes

Natural minor has a triad on ♭7̂ and none on 7̂. Harmonic minor has a triad on 7̂ and none on ♭7̂. Those are two different degrees, a whole step apart, and each form of the scale has only one of them. In the table that's the two cells that stay empty.

Three triads change quality between the two forms, and all three contain 7̂. In C minor: ♭III, E♭ G B♭, becomes ♭III+, E♭ G B♮. The triad on 5̂ goes from v, G B♭ D, to V, G B♮ D. And ♭VII, B♭ D F, becomes vii°, B♮ D F.

quality in words

Major, minor, diminished, augmented. Perfect describes fourths, fifths, octaves and unisons, never a triad.

Chord symbols like Cm7 or B♭7 carry the root and the quality but have no slot for the inversion. Where a question asks for root, quality, inversion and figures, a symbol answers half of it.

the same three pitches in two keys

Part 7 closes by asking which two of its twelve chords are the same three pitches in different keys. They're numbers 4 and 9: A, C and E♭, with C in the bass both times.

A C E♭ is a diminished triad. In B♭ major it stands on 7̂, so it's vii°6. In G minor it stands on 2̂, so it's ii°6. The sound is identical and the function is not: vii°6 pulls to the tonic a semitone above it, ii°6 goes on to the dominant.
